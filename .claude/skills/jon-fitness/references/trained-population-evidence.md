# Evidence in trained and well-trained populations

Most resistance-training research uses untrained or recreationally trained adults, and "trained" is defined
differently in each study (as little as ≥ 6 months to ≥ 1 year of lifting; elite athletes are rarer still). This file
collects the studies that speak to **trained lifters and athletes** so the skill's rules for experienced clients rest on
the right population, and it says plainly where **no study of highly strength-trained people exists**.

Abstracts below were retrieved from Europe PMC on 2026-09-20 (PMID given); full papers were not opened. Effect sizes
are quoted from the abstracts. "Trained" follows each paper's own definition.

## 1. Studies by question

### Dose: frequency, intensity, volume
| Study (population) | Finding | Implication for the skill |
|---|---|---|
| Rhea 2003, meta-analysis, 140 studies (PMID 12618576) | Maximal gains at ~80% 1RM in **trained** vs ~60% untrained; trained ~2 d/wk per muscle group vs 3 untrained; 4 sets per muscle group | V5 trains each lift 2×/wk at 80% then 85–100%, in line with the trained optimum |
| Peterson 2004, meta-analysis, 37 studies, **competitive athletes** (15142003) | Maximal strength gains at ~85% 1RM, 2 d/wk, ~8 sets per muscle group; differs from non-athletes | Same: 2×/wk and ≥ 85% in the intensification weeks (V5 wk 5–9) |
| Ralston 2017, meta-analysis, 9 studies (28755103) | Higher weekly sets gave slightly larger strength gains than low (ES 0.18, 95% CI 0.06–0.30) | The dose-response means are not caps; V5's weekly heavy sets per lift (12 in wk 1–4 falling to 7 in wk 9) sit at or above them |
| Baz-Valle 2022, systematic review, participants with **≥ 1 year** of training (35291645) | 12–20 weekly sets per muscle group suggested for hypertrophy in young trained men; moderate vs high volume no different for quadriceps or biceps, high better for triceps | For size goals: 12–20 sets per muscle per week, set from the log |
| Heaselgrave 2019, RCT, **resistance-experienced** men, biceps only (30160627) | 9 weekly sets once a week raised muscle thickness; 18–27 sets across 2 sessions may give larger strength gains | Volume matters more than the day-split; more sets can help strength |
| Hamarsland 2021, RCT, trained participants, n = 21, 9 weeks (35069251) | Volume-equated 2× vs 4×/wk: no difference in muscle growth or strength | Frequency is a scheduling choice once volume is equal |
| Schoenfeld 2017, meta-analysis (28834797) | Heavy loads give greater 1RM strength; hypertrophy similar across loads when sets go to failure | Heavy anchors for strength; lighter accessories for size |

### Effort and autoregulation
| Study | Finding | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Helms 2018, RCT, **resistance-trained** males, 8 wk (29628895) | RPE-based vs %1RM loading with matched sets/reps: both effective; RPE group had a small, non-significant 1RM advantage | Keep %1RM as the wave with an RPE/RIR layer on top |
| Zourdos 2016, **experienced** (5.2 yr) vs novice squatters (26049792) | RPE/RIR scale tracked bar velocity strongly (r = −0.88 experienced); experienced lifters rated 1RM higher and were more efficient at high loads | RIR-based RPE is usable in experienced lifters and in the 1RM test |
| Refalo 2023, meta-analysis, **any experience** (36334240) | No evidence that training to momentary failure beats non-failure for hypertrophy (ES 0.12, CI −0.13 to 0.37); trivial advantage for "set failure" | Failure is not required; accessories at RIR 1–3 are enough |

### Concurrent training (strength + conditioning)
| Study | Finding | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Petré 2021, 27 studies (33751469) | Lower-body 1RM fell with concurrent training in **trained** (ES −0.35) but not moderately trained or untrained people; larger same-session; trained lifters should separate modes when strength is the priority | Separate the conditioning session (options A/C); it is the trained-lifter rule |
| Sabag 2018, meta-analysis, HIIT + resistance training (29658408) | Lower-body strength gain lower with concurrent HIIT (ES −0.248, p = 0.049); **cycling HIIT trended worse (−0.377, p = 0.074) than running (−0.176, ns)**; longer inter-modal rest may reduce the effect | **"Bike, not running" is not supported for HIIT.** Modality is unresolved; spacing the modes is the better-supported lever |
| Ferraro-Farro 2026, 9 RCTs, 177 healthy adults (Europe PMC) | Sprint-interval + resistance training showed no significant strength difference vs resistance training alone; VO₂max higher; short sprints (≤ 10 s) gave greater jump gains | Sprint intervals are a reasonable conditioning form; trial populations were not highly trained |
| Huiberts 2024 (per `research_notes/`, not opened) | No classified study involved highly strength-trained participants | The strength-trained gap is real |

### Recovery weeks, peaking
| Study | Finding | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Coleman 2024, RCT, **resistance-trained** men and women, 9 wk (38274324) | A full week off mid-programme did **not** improve later gains; lower-body isometric and dynamic strength improved **more** with continuous training; no effect on hypertrophy, power or endurance | Do not prescribe a complete-rest deload for a trained lifter; use reduced load and autoregulated triggers (week 10 = reduced-load test week) |
| Travis 2020, review of tapering and peaking for powerlifting (32917000) | Evidence for peaking maximal strength in strength athletes is lacking and details are thin | The V5 taper/meet options are practice-based, not evidence-based |
| Seitz & Haff 2016, meta-analysis, 47 studies (26508319) | Post-activation potentiation is larger in **stronger, more experienced** lifters; plyometric conditioning activities ES 0.47; longer recovery intervals give larger effects | Potentiation is more plausible in well-trained lifters; a jump primer before *squatting* is not a test of it (potentiation is measured on later jumps/sprints) |

### Getting lean while training (the "shredded" branch)
| Study | Finding | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Garthe 2011, RCT, **elite athletes**, n = 24 (21558571) | Slower loss (0.7%/wk) increased lean mass (+2.1%) vs unchanged with faster loss (1.4%/wk) | Prefer the slower rate; the rate is a nutrition decision for the client/RD |
| Murphy & Koehler 2022, meta-analysis (34623696) | Energy deficit impaired lean-mass gains (ES −0.57) but strength gains were comparable; deficits > 500 kcal/day prevented lean-mass gains | Strength can be held in a deficit; muscle gain is what suffers. **Conflicts with the ISA band of 500–1000 kcal/day for someone protecting muscle — prefer the low end and defer to the RD** |
| Helms 2014, systematic review, **resistance-trained lean athletes** (24092765) | Higher protein intakes in restriction (recommended 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass) | Research context only; **the skill does not prescribe macros** — refer to a registered dietitian |

## 2. What is still missing for well-trained lifters
- No concurrent-training trial in **highly** strength-trained people; sprint-interval results are from healthy adults.
- Peaking and tapering for strength athletes is under-researched (Travis 2020).
- "Trained" is defined inconsistently (6 months to > 5 years), so effect sizes cannot be matched to a specific client without judgement.
- Nothing here validates the exact sprint dose, the week-10 design or the placement rules; they are judgement built on these studies.

## 3. Classifying the client (intake)
Record **strength experience and conditioning experience separately** (P19) and treat a client as "trained" for these rules
if they have **≥ 1 year of consistent progressive resistance training** (the inclusion used by Baz-Valle 2022), noting
that several key papers use stricter definitions. State the definition used in the plan's evidence section.
